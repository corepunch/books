# Craft: what makes an illustrated adventure interesting

Read this before stage 1 and again before stages 2, 6 and 11. It adapts what
readers remember from Infocom's games and the Zork gamebooks to picture books
with choices. The stages say *what* to produce; this says what makes it good.

The reference is *Zork: The Forces of Krill*. Measured page by page it has
about twenty-five distinct places, 19 decision pages, 53 plain "turn the page"
pages, 20 endings (most of them failures) and only three pages that check
something the heroes found earlier. That is the shape of an adventure: a
journey with a lot of story, frequent forks, real danger and many ways to fail.
It is not a puzzle set in one room.

## 1. A journey, not a room

An adventure takes the hero somewhere. Krill starts at a school, passes through
a magic portal, foothills, a deserted village, a forest, a white house, a
cellar, a troll's lair, a cliff, a river, a dam, a bridge, a coal mine and a bat's
nest, and ends in the warlock's throne room.

- **Forward motion.** Almost every decision leads somewhere new. The hero rarely
  goes back; when a path returns, it is because the story sends them (they are
  chased, captured, carried by the river).
- **Many places, each distinct.** A short book still has six or more locations,
  and no single location holds more than a quarter of the pages. Each place
  has one strong visual anchor the reader can name: "the signpost at the fork",
  "the white house with the boarded door", "the rope bridge".
- **The hero ends far from the start.** Draw the route as a line on a map; it
  must go somewhere.
- **Scenery changes the mood.** Sunny path, dark forest, roaring river,
  underground maze. Each new place is a reward in itself.
- **A place may be a small cluster** (a house with kitchen, living room and
  cellar), explored for a few pages before the road continues. A cluster is an
  episode, never the whole book.
- Keep geography consistent and mappable: the falls seen from the cliff are the
  falls the raft is swept toward later.

## 2. A quest with stakes

The pitch names a fantasy of play *and* stakes: "two children carry a magic
sword across an invaded kingdom to the one wizard who can use it". Give the
book:

- **A goal far away** (deliver, rescue, escape, reach, warn).
- **An opposing force** that acts on its own: a warlock, a storm, a flood, a
  hunter, the coming night. It shows up along the road, not only at the end.
- **A reason to hurry or a reason to be afraid**, set on the first pages.
- **Companions and strangers.** A pair of heroes gives dialogue and
  disagreement ("I say the dark passage." "I say ford the river."). Strangers
  along the way — the knight, the old man, the hermit, the frog, the gnome —
  give advice, lie, help or trick.

Do not build the story around picking up and carrying things; in these
books the hero never takes or keeps objects. What the book remembers is what
happened: a warning heard, a stranger befriended, a shortcut seen from the
tree. Krill's bronze key becomes, here, "the songbird showed Juranda the hidden
latch".

## 3. What the reader decides

Most choices in Krill are about the road and the people on it. Use these, in
roughly this proportion:

- **Which way** (about half): the leafy trail or the dirt trail; the boat or
  the river bank; the stairs, the passage or the ford.
- **Whom to trust** (about a quarter): follow the knights or find the old man;
  listen to Bivotar, Juranda or the frog; meet the friendly stranger or run.
- **Risk or safety** (about a quarter): take the sword or walk away; fight the
  troll or flee; jump off the cliff after the knight; save the treasure or
  yourself.

Small reasoning challenges are seasoning, not the meal:

- **Warnings to remember.** The old man says "avoid the trail of leaves"; many
  pages later the trail forks. The reader who remembers survives.
- **Noticing.** The waterfall seen from the air is ahead of the raft.
- **Understanding a character.** Knowing what a creature fears or wants.
- **Something that happened earlier.** A stranger helped, or the hero saw a
  way in from the treetop, so a later door opens. Keep these to two or three
  in the whole book.

Rules:

- The information needed for a good choice is in the world before the choice,
  in the text (and ideally the picture). Afterwards the reader thinks "of
  course" or "I should have listened", never "how could I know?".
- Some choices are honest gambles with no clue. Those may lead to danger, but
  not to an ending without warning in the text or picture ("the approaching
  figure seems friendly... Bivotar grasps the sword").
- A puzzle never stops the story. No page asks the reader to try the same
  thing until it works.

## 4. Choices that matter

- Every choice is a decision with a distinct outcome. Two captions that lead to
  the same page are one choice.
- Two or three choices per decision page. One is not a choice; four overwhelms
  the picture.
- Set every option up in the text, then offer it as a short question under its
  circle, as the Zork books do (see [CHOICES.md](CHOICES.md)).
- When branches reconverge, the rejoined page acknowledges the path taken.
- A wrong choice changes the story: a detour, a danger, a capture, an early
  ending. It is never "nothing happens, try again".

## 5. Pacing: story between the forks

Krill has almost three story pages for every decision page. A choice lands
harder after a page of travel, a stranger's speech, or a chase.

- Put a decision every two or three pages, not on every page.
- Continue pages move the story: arrive somewhere, meet someone, hear news,
  see danger coming. They are not "you walk to the next room".
- Alternate travel, encounter and decision, and let the tension rise toward
  each act's end.

## 6. Danger, failure and trying again

Failure endings are part of the form. Krill has twenty endings; the heroes are
captured, eaten by grues, swept over the falls, or simply go home and watch
TV. Each is short, telegraphed, sometimes funny, and followed by "go back and
try again".

- Plan several failure endings (four or more in a short book), spread across
  the journey, each caused by a choice the reader could judge.
- Warn first: a sound, a smell, a character's advice, a picture of what lies
  ahead.
- Match the audience. For young readers a failure is gentle or comic (lost in
  the fog until morning, sent home by a grumpy giant, a long nap), never gory.
  It still ends the adventure.
- Offer a "safe" choice that ends the book early and dull (stay with the old
  man; ignore the sword). It shows the reader what courage is for.
- Rank endings: one full success, one or two partial successes, several
  failures. Krill scores each ending out of ten; the page text can say how far
  the hero got instead.

## 7. Tone: wonder, danger and humour together

- Mix the mundane and the magical: hot-pepper sandwiches in a wizard's kitchen,
  a hermit who misses picking up bones.
- Let danger feel real even when it is gentle.
- Humour comes from characters and situations, next to straight moments.
- In each act include something beautiful, something funny and something warm.

## 8. Things with identity

The hero does not carry things, but the world is full of things with identity
that the hero uses where they stand: a boat controlled by words, a rope bridge
with one frayed rope, a trophy case with runes, a note nailed to a signpost.
Give each more than one role: route, clue, joke, risk, memory.

## 9. Characters with behaviour

The heroes argue and disagree, which lets the reader side with one. Strangers
act: they help, deceive, chase, transform, guide. Give each important
character a want and a visible change (the friendly stranger becomes a bird of
prey). Keep dialogue short and in the page text.

## 10. Acts and thresholds

Three acts, each with a different main activity and a different stretch of
country:

1. **Departure:** the call, the crossing into the adventure world, the first
   strangers, the first fork. Ends when there is no easy way home.
2. **The road:** the longest act; several locations, dangers and branches.
   Ends when the goal is in sight or the enemy strikes.
3. **The confrontation:** the lair, the final crossing, the rescue. Ends in
   success, partial success or failure.

After each threshold the world is different: new country, new danger, news
that changes what the heroes want.

## 11. Openings and endings

- **Opening:** start in the ordinary world, then let something strange happen
  within the first pages (a glowing sword in a bush). The first decision
  arrives by page two or three, and the first new place by page three or four.
- **Good ending:** resolves the quest, recalls specific places and choices
  from the road, and points onward. Infocom's best trick: end the story walking
  toward a new place, and begin the sequel exactly there.

## 12. Secrets and replay

Different branches visit different places, so no single read sees the whole
world. Add at least one hidden place, one shortcut and one kinder way through.
Readers return to find what they missed and to reach the best ending.

## 13. Brevity and imagination

Keep each page's text short and concrete. The illustration shows the place;
the text gives what the picture cannot: a sound, a smell, a thought, a line of
dialogue. Leave room for the reader's imagination.

## 14. Social reading

Design for a parent and child arguing about the choice: which trail, which
stranger, fight or run. Parent notes (stage 10) help an adult guide without
spoiling.

## Checklist

- [ ] The route goes somewhere: six or more locations, the hero ends far from
      the start, and no location holds more than a quarter of the pages.
- [ ] The pitch names a fantasy of play, a goal far away and an opposing force.
- [ ] Most choices are about the road, trust or risk; reasoning puzzles are
      few and never stall the story.
- [ ] Roughly two story pages for every decision page.
- [ ] Several failure endings, each warned and caused by a judgeable choice,
      plus one early "safe and dull" ending.
- [ ] Choices are decisions with distinct outcomes; reconvergence
      acknowledges the path.
- [ ] Every location has a nameable anchor, and the map could be drawn.
- [ ] Strangers along the way help, deceive or change.
- [ ] Each act has its own country, and something beautiful, funny and warm.
- [ ] The good ending recalls specific places and choices and points onward.
- [ ] Branches visit different places; there is a secret and a shortcut.
