# Craft: what makes an illustrated adventure interesting

Read this before stage 1 and again before stages 2, 6 and 11. It adapts what
players remember from Infocom's games and the Zork gamebooks to picture books
with choices. The stages say *what* to produce; this says what makes it good.

## 1. A world, not a flowchart

Readers remember Zork as a *place*: the white house, the dam, the maze. Build
the same feeling.

- Give every location one strong visual anchor the reader can name: "the desk
  under the window", "the rope bridge", "the owl's hollow".
- Keep geography stable and mappable. A reader should be able to draw the map
  after one read. Reuse hubs and safe places.
- Give reasons to return. A location revisited after a key event must look or
  read differently.
- Let things persist: a door left open stays open, a lamp knocked over stays
  on the floor.

## 2. A high concept beyond "find the things"

The pitch must name a fantasy of play, not just a quest: "a kitten crossing a
giant's attic in the dark", "guiding a baby dragon who melts ice at the worst
moments", "a detective who solves the case by noticing". Do not build the
story around picking up and carrying things. Goals are about reaching a
place, helping someone, finding out the truth, escaping, or deciding who to
trust. When the hero does take something, it is one story moment that a later
page remembers as a fact, never a collection.

## 3. Challenges are the memory engine

In a parser game the puzzles are what players remember. A choice book has no
typing, so its challenges come from noticing, remembering and understanding:

- **Noticing:** the answer is visible in a picture or said in the text before
  the choice ("the rope's frayed end" painted on the bridge two pages earlier).
- **Remembering:** a fact from earlier makes one choice sensible (the owl said
  the path by the birches is safe).
- **Understanding a character:** knowing what the dragon can do, or what the
  grumpy troll wants, tells the reader which choice works.
- **Order and timing:** doing things in a sensible order (light the lamp
  before entering the cellar).

Rules:

- The answer must already be in the world before the choice. Afterwards the
  reader should think "of course", never "how could I know?".
- A clue may be in the picture, but it must also be in the text or in an
  earlier picture seen up close, so a reader who misses a detail can still
  reason it out.
- Use small challenges early to teach how the book works.
- Vary the kinds: not every challenge is a locked door and a key. Use light,
  height, water, weight, animals, characters, sound and time.
- Make progress visible: a new place opens, the picture changes, a character
  reacts.

## 4. Choices that matter

- Every choice is a decision the reader can reason about: safety or
  curiosity, help or hurry, trust or doubt. Never offer two choices that lead
  to the same outcome.
- Two or three choices per room page. One is too few; four overwhelms the
  picture.
- Set every option up in the text, then offer it as a short question under
  its circle, as the Zork books do (see [CHOICES.md](CHOICES.md)).
- When branches reconverge, the rejoined page should acknowledge the path
  taken (a line of text or a detail in the picture), so choices feel real.
- A "wrong" choice teaches something: a funny setback, a new clue, a small
  danger survived. Never punish a choice the reader had no way to judge.

## 5. Fair difficulty, low cruelty

- Challenge comes from reasoning, not from hidden, irreversible mistakes.
- Warn before real danger in the text or picture.
- For young readers, bad outcomes are recoverable: the hero slips back, tries
  again, or takes the long way. Bad endings are allowed only when the design
  says so, and must be telegraphed and funny or gentle.

## 6. Tone: wonder, danger and humour together

- Mix the mundane and the magical: a kitchen with a talking kettle.
- Let danger feel real even when it is gentle: a creaking floorboard, a dark
  stairwell, a gust that blows out the candle.
- Make humour part of play: give silly or curious choices an amusing short
  outcome that returns the hero ("Mira sniffs the ink. Achoo!"). Comedy lands
  better next to straight moments.
- In each third of the book, include something beautiful, something funny and
  something warm.

## 7. Objects with several roles

Every important object should serve at least two of: a practical use, a clue,
worldbuilding, a joke, a risk, a reward, a memory marker. Give keys and
treasures identity (a bronze key in a bird's nest, not "a key"). Put story into
things rather than exposition: a letter on the desk, a scratched height chart
on the door frame.

## 8. Characters with behaviour

Memorable characters act. Give each one at least three states the reader can
see and affect (asleep, suspicious, helpful), and let them move, block, help,
follow or react to what the hero did earlier. Keep dialogue short and in the
page text.

## 9. Acts and thresholds

Structure the book in three acts with a clear threshold between them, each
with a different main activity: exploring, then working things out, then a
confrontation or rescue. After each threshold, at least two locations change
in picture or text. The world responds to what the hero did.

## 10. Openings and endings

- **Opening:** one landmark, one visible goal, one obstacle, and one success
  within the first two choices.
- **Ending:** resolves the goal, recalls at least two specific discoveries or
  choices, and leaves a feeling of what comes next. Infocom's best trick: end
  the story walking toward a new place, and begin the sequel exactly there.

## 11. Secrets and replay

Add at least one optional secret (a hidden place, an extra detail in a picture
that pays off, a kinder way through) and at least one alternative route or
order. Readers return to find what they missed.

## 12. Brevity and imagination

Keep page text short and concrete. The illustration shows the room; the text
gives the one detail the picture cannot (a sound, a smell, a thought). Leave
room for the reader's imagination, as Infocom's prose did.

## 13. Social reading

Design for a parent and child talking about the page: choices worth arguing
over, pictures worth pointing at, and names easy to say aloud. Parent notes
(stage 10) help an adult guide without spoiling.

## Checklist

- [ ] The pitch names a fantasy of play, not only a quest.
- [ ] Every location has a nameable anchor, and the map could be drawn.
- [ ] Each challenge's answer is visible or stated before its choice.
- [ ] Choices are decisions with distinct outcomes; reconvergence
      acknowledges the path.
- [ ] Mistakes are recoverable or clearly telegraphed.
- [ ] Each act has something beautiful, funny and warm.
- [ ] Important objects have at least two roles.
- [ ] Characters have visible states and react to earlier choices.
- [ ] Thresholds change at least two locations.
- [ ] The ending recalls specific discoveries and points onward.
- [ ] There is at least one secret and one alternative route.
